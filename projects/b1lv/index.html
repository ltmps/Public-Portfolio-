Preparing a liquid rocket for testing requires a clear understanding of its components, potential failures and expected behavior. On the Bronco 1 Propellant Feed Systems team, I helped connect hardware records, modeling assumptions and test evidence.

My contributionTest evidenceLessonsSources

My contribution

Keep the hardware story traceable.

My work centered on shared technical reporting, project records and inventory. These supported the team's understanding of what was assembled, what changed and what still needed investigation.

Hardware records

I maintained meeting notes, documented important design changes and helped manage inventory through the Hardware Utilization List.

Keeping these records current helped connect project decisions to the hardware configuration being discussed.

Technical reporting

I contributed to shared report sections covering piping assembly and cleaning, tank fatigue analysis, water-flow testing, and failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA).

These were team deliverables. My reporting contributions do not imply that I personally performed every test or model described in them.

Failure analysis

I was a shared author of the piping FMEA. The work connected potential component failures with their effects on the wider system, helping frame the team's test-readiness discussions.

Team evidence

Read the data alongside the hardware.

The team characterized pressure, temperature and mass flow using instrumented replica piping on a mobile test stand, without the engine. This water-flow test provided evidence about the feed system, rather than a flight result.

Measurements need context.

The team's presentation records leakage and inconsistencies in the data. A run was scrubbed and repeated after the leaks were addressed. The resulting lesson is to interpret a trend alongside the condition of the hardware and the uncertainty in the measurements.

A model is a different kind of evidence.

A complementary tank study compared SolidWorks fatigue analysis with hand calculations under pressure and thermal loading. Those results were model predictions, not measurements of service life. I contributed to the shared reporting, rather than claiming sole ownership of the analysis.

How to read this evidence

The graph is a team analysis figure, not a new analysis performed for this portfolio. It does not establish flight performance, a reliability improvement or measured tank service life. The documented test issues are part of the evidence and are retained in the narrative.

What I learned

Make assumptions visible.

My personal reflection in the final report emphasized looking beyond nominal predictions, understanding the system behind a sensor reading, and recording changes as they happened.

Examine uncertainty.

With limited experiments, a nominal result is only part of the picture. I learned to examine the assumptions and uncertainty behind an analysis before treating it as a decision-making input.

Understand the measurement path.

Piping and instrumentation diagrams, component behavior and the test environment provide context for sensor readings. Understanding that context matters when interpreting unexpected data.

Record changes while they are fresh.

Meeting notes, design-change records and inventory tracking preserve the reasoning behind a configuration. I learned to treat documentation as part of the engineering work throughout the project.
